## psychodynamic:1 - Engaging in a Therapeutic Inquiry (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. For the beginner patient statements: Ask about missing details in the narrative or clarify a vague or confusing part of the narrative.
1. For the intermediate patient statements: Ask about how the patient comprehends or understands a part of the narrative.
2. For the advanced patient statements: Ask about the implications of the narrative for the future.

## psychodynamic:3 - Deepening Emotional Experience (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Use one or more of the following to intensify the most poignant feeling and meaning embedded within the patient’s statement:
1. Option 1: Use vivid language and match your tone of voice and manner to the patient’s underlying emotion.
2. Option 2: Use an “I” statement spoken as if it is from the patient’s point of view.
3. Option 3: Use a metaphor or analogy.

## psychodynamic:4 - Making Process Comments (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Ask permission to take a look at what happened right now between the therapist and patient.
1. Name what just transpired between therapist and patient.
2. Option 1: Invite the patient to explore their feelings about what transpired. or Option 2: Conjecture about the patient’s feelings about what transpired.

## psychodynamic:5 - Pointing Out Defenses and Inquiring About Underlying Fear (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Point out the defensive strategy to the patient in an empathic and validating manner.
1. Inquire or wonder out loud what the patient fears might happen if they did not use the defensive strategy.

## psychodynamic:6 - Introducing the Rationale for Treatment (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Validate or express appreciation for the patient’s concern, question, or statement.
1. Reflect the core element(s) behind the patient’s concern.
2. Give a rationale for why that element is important.
3. Ask how the patient feels about what you’ve said and/or if they find it relevant.

## psychodynamic:7 - Making Transference Interpretations (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Start by internally (silently) considering if what the patient has said about, felt about, or done with others parallels what is happening in your relationship with the patient.
1. Reflect back what the patient has conveyed about their experience with others.
2. Offer a brief, tactful reflection that summarizes a possible parallel with you, the therapist.
3. Ask an open-ended question about what the patient thinks or feels about what you have said.

## psychodynamic:8 - Using Metaphors (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. For the beginner patient statements: Ask a question using aspects of the patient’s metaphor that would likely prompt them to expand their feelings or understanding in some way.
1. For the intermediate patient statements: Tentatively say the implicit message behind the metaphor back to the patient. Finish by asking if that sounds right.
2. For the advanced patient statements: Tentatively use a metaphor that captures the meaning of something the patient is saying, doing, or feeling that would be likely to click into focus their feelings or understandings in some way. Finish by asking about the usefulness of the metaphor.
3. Remember Isaac Newton’s definition of saying things in a tactful manner from Exercise 7.

## psychodynamic:9 - Exploring Fantasy (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Identify and name the conflict between the patient’s present circumstances and what they ideally want.
1. Name the implicit or explicit fantasy.
2. Ask if the fantasy resolves the conflict.

## psychodynamic:11 - Using Supervision to Recognize Reenactments (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Share with your clinical supervisor your complementary countertransferential response(s) to something that the patient has said or done.
1. Specify what the patient said or did to evoke that reaction.
2. State the urge you had to say or do something in the moment in response to the patient.

## psychodynamic:12 - Providing a Corrective Emotional Experience (Psychodynamic Psychotherapy)
0. Mention the way the patient had to adapt to caregivers growing up (i.e., acts of self-protection) by forfeiting a healthy and normal part of the self to remain connected.
1. Describe how glimmers of this healthy part show up in the patient’s present statements, feelings, or actions.
2. Affirm why this healthy part is a good (helpful) thing.
3. Inquire how the patient felt about showing this previously forfeited part of the self.

## rebt:1 - Psychoeducation About Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy’s ABC Model (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Using content from the client’s statement, educate the client on the REBT ABC model.
1. Check for the client’s understanding of the REBT ABC model.

## rebt:2 - Psychoeducation About Dysfunctional Versus Functional Negative Emotions and Behaviors (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Teach the client that their UNE interferes with achieving their goals and elicits behaviors that are dysfunctional.
1. Teach the client an alternative HNE that helps them achieve their goals and elicits adaptive behaviors.
2. Propose that the goal of therapy be to replace a UNE with an HNE.

## rebt:3 - Agreement on the Session Goals (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Clarify the client’s statement by translating it into a goal that represents both of the following components: Component A: The replacement of an unhealthy negative emotion (e.g., depression) with a healthy negative emotion (e.g., sadness) Component B: The reduction or elimination of a dysfunctional behavior (e.g., social avoidance) by the initiation or increase of a new adaptive behavior (e.g., social engagement)
1. Ask for the client’s agreement on a proposed goal.

## rebt:4 - Clarifying Inferences From Irrational Beliefs (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Reflect the initial inferential thoughts presented by the client.
1. Reflect the initial irrational beliefs presented by the client.
2. Offer psychoeducation to differentiate inferences from irrational beliefs.
3. Check with the client to see whether they understand that the irrational demand or evaluative irrational belief will be the target for change.

## rebt:5 - Assessing Irrational Beliefs About the Activating Event (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Propose the existence of a demand as well as any derivative evaluative irrational beliefs.
1. Distinguish these demands and evaluative irrational beliefs and their consequences from those of the rational beliefs.

## rebt:6 - Prioritizing Which Irrational Beliefs to Target for Change (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Reflect the main irrational beliefs presented by the client.
1. Highlight the one irrational belief that appears to be the primary contributor to the unhealthy consequence.
2. Check with the client for agreement that this belief represents the primary irrational belief to be targeted for change.

## rebt:7 - Teaching the Belief–Consequence Connection (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Make a statement that connects the client’s irrational belief with its consequence (C).
1. Ask a question that checks the client’s understanding of the B–C connection.

## rebt:8 - Functional Disputation of Irrational Beliefs (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Highlight the main irrational beliefs presented by the client.
1. Challenge the client’s irrational beliefs using a functional approach.
2. Check that the client understands that their irrational beliefs are working against their clinical goals.

## rebt:9 - Empirical Disputation of Irrational Beliefs (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Highlight the main irrational belief presented by the client.
1. Challenge the client’s irrational belief using an empirical approach.
2. Check if the client understands that their irrational belief is inconsistent with reality.

## rebt:10 - Semantic Disputation of Irrational Beliefs (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Highlight the main irrational belief presented by the client.
1. Challenge the meaning of the client’s irrational belief.
2. Using content from the example, the clinician Socratically challenges the concrete irrational belief.

## rebt:11 - Constructing Full Rational Alternative Beliefs to Replace Irrational Beliefs (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. Formulate the full alternative rational belief(s).
1. Check if the client sees how the new full alternative rational belief(s) result in a healthy negative emotion and adaptive behavior.

## rebt:12 - Collaborative Homework Development (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy)
0. The clinician proposes a homework assignment with the client that is aligned with clinical goals.
1. The clinician asks the client to set a specific day, time, and place to complete the homework.
2. The clinician assesses the presence of any practical, emotional, or cognitive obstacles that would make homework completion more challenging and less likely.

## schema:1 - Understanding and Attunement (Schema Therapy)
0. Communicate an empathic understanding of the clientʼs “internal reality.”
1. Reassure the client about your desire to understand and be helpful to them.
2. Nonverbal: Use a soft, warm voice and slight movement toward the client.

## schema:2 - Supporting and Strengthening the Healthy Adult Mode (Schema Therapy)
0. Support the clientʼs autonomy by pointing out healthy adult mode behaviors (e.g., making decisions, setting limits, acting to get a need met in a healthy way).
1. Validate and praise evidence of the healthy adult mode (e.g., displays of competence, courage, achievement, limit-setting, self-advocacy, assertiveness).

## schema:3 - Schema Education: Beginning to Understand Current Problems in Schema Therapy Terms (Schema Therapy)
0. Suggest the presence of a pattern in the clientʼs life, and call this pattern a schema.
1. Explain the origin of schemas in terms of early unmet needs.
2. Educate the client about how schemas are strong emotional beliefs about the self, others, and the world.

## schema:4 - Linking Unmet Needs, Schema, and Presenting Problem (Schema Therapy)
0. Validate the importance of needs that were unmet in the clientʼs childhood experience.
1. Suggest a link between the clientʼs early unmet needs and the development of a schema.
2. Suggest a link between the clientʼs schema and possible problems in their adult life.

## schema:5 - Education About Maladaptive Schema Modes (Schema Therapy)
0. Point out the clientʼs behaviors that suggest a maladaptive mode has been triggered.
1. Explain the basic definition of schema modes in terms of parts of self or transient states that are triggered when schemas are activated.

## schema:6 - Recognizing the Mode Shifts of the Maladaptive Coping Modes (Schema Therapy)
0. Point out a shift or emotional reaction in the clientʼs behavior.
1. Inquire about the clientʼs ability to recognize this shift.
2. Raise the possibility that a coping mode has been triggered. (You do not need to identify the specific type of coping mode being triggered.)

## schema:7 - Identifying the Presence of the Demanding/Punitive Inner Critic Mode (Schema Therapy)
0. Point out that the critic mode may be activated.
1. Point out the clientʼs overly demanding or punitive self-criticism.
2. Inquire about the possible origins of the critic mode in childhood and adolescence.

## schema:8 - Identifying the Presence of the Angry and Vulnerable Child Modes (Schema Therapy)
0. Gently point out the clientʼs emotional intensity.
1. Inquire about the clientʼs ability to recognize this emotional state.
2. Raise the possibility that this emotional state represents the triggering of an angry or vulnerable child mode.

## schema:9 - Limited Reparenting for the Angry and Vulnerable Child Modes (Schema Therapy)
0. Validate the clientʼs emotional expression and triggering of their child mode as understandable given their early unmet needs.
1. Take one of the following actions to meet the need within professional boundaries: **Action 1:** Remind the client of your connection and support. **Action 2:** Encourage the clientʼs emotional expression. **Action 3:** Suggest an imagery exercise that meets the need.

## schema:10 - Limited Reparenting for the Demanding/Punitive Inner Critic Mode (Schema Therapy)
0. Point out the inner critic mode and speculate about its childhood origins.
1. Challenge the message of the critic as being inaccurate, untrue, biased, or unfair.
2. Suggest an alternative “good parent” message to meet the clientʼs present need.

## schema:11 - Limited Reparenting for the Maladaptive Coping Modes: Empathic Confrontation (Schema Therapy)
0. Point out in a warm, noncritical way how a coping mode behavior is problematic because it does not get the clientʼs need met.
1. Communicate the understanding that this is a leftover survival response from childhood when a core need was not met.
2. Suggest that an alternative behavior is necessary to get the clientʼs need met.

## schema:12 - Implementing Behavioral Pattern Breaking Through Homework Assignments (Schema Therapy)
0. Suggest one of the following behavioral pattern-breaking assignments that the client can try during their week: Assignment 1: Written or audio/video flash cards
1. Explain how this assignment helps continue the work that took place during the session.

## systemic:1 - Providing a Systemic Rationale for Treatment (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Acknowledge and validate the presenting issue as the person sees it.
1. Provide a systemic rationale as to the potential benefits of a systemic, relational approach.
2. Check the fit of the systemic rationale with the client(s) using an open-ended question.

## systemic:2 - Building the Therapeutic Alliance: Establishing Bonds and Joining the System (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Explicitly convey that you are listening to each system member’s unique view of the problem or session content.
1. With each system member, identify and reflect relevant emotions or meaning in a warm, understanding, and accepting way.
2. (a) Seek feedback from the system members on their experience of you and the therapy process.

## systemic:3 - Reframing the Problem (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Reflect and validate your understanding of the client’s point of view in a way that is supportive, direct, clear, and nonblaming. In a couple or family, it is necessary to validate all perspectives before providing a reframe.
1. Provide an alternative way (i.e., frame) of looking at the problem, relationship, or situation that is plausible and believable for each client present.
2. Check with the client(s) to evaluate the fit of the frame.

## systemic:4 - Interactional Structuring: Deescalation (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Make a statement that interrupts the couple/family from their current negative interaction or links current interaction to their pattern.
1. Validate the importance of the issue or the underlying needs for all parties involved.
2. Set a specific boundary related to what the clients say (e.g., no name-calling, blaming, interrupting, yelling) and how they say it (everything goes through the therapist).

## systemic:5 - Avoiding Triangulation (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Demonstrate empathy toward the idea being expressed by the client.
1. Redirect the focus back to the primary dyadic relationship (where the stress is).
2. Ask an open-ended question that keeps the focus on actionable ways to improve the primary dyadic relationship.

## systemic:6 - Highlighting Boundaries (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Reflect the boundary communicated in the client statement.
1. Ask the couple or family to turn to one another and talk directly about a boundaryrelated issue (two-way enactment).

## systemic:7 - Systemic Questions: Fostering Perspective-Taking (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Offer a brief reflection based on the client’s previous statement.
1. Ask an open-ended question that invites the client to take on the perspective of another person in terms of their emotions, cognitions, or behaviors in the present moment or in the future.
2. The question should not ask about something that already happened (e.g., “What did your partner feel, think, or do when you experienced that?”).

## systemic:8 - Engendering Hope (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. State a brief reflection or validate a specific feeling based on the client statement that conveys understanding of their perspective of the problem at hand.
1. Offer an alternative perspective of their feeling or position, built on a strength you heard.
2. Check in with the client to see how the perspective you offered connects to their goals or vision of how things might be different.

## systemic:9 - Attention to Diversity (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Convey a brief empathic response to a client/family member that indicates you hear their problem or the session content.
1. Reflect on problem/session content, looking for opportunities to deepen the conversation as it relates to their identity and unique experiences.
2. Ask a question that conveys continued empathy and curiosity about their identity and unique experiences with the problem.

## systemic:10 - Building the Therapeutic Alliance: Establishing Therapeutic Goals (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. The therapist provides a brief, empathic summary or reflection of the presenting problem or context.
1. The therapist solicits goals from each member of the system (using the methods suggested (e.g., miracle question).
2. The therapist summarizes the goals from each system member.
3. The therapist suggests a goal, leaving it open for system members to agree or disagree.

## systemic:11 - Tracking the Interactional Cycle (Systemic Family Therapy)
0. Implicit skill, not verbally stated: Identify what elements of the cycle are being highlighted in the client prompt (i.e., Person 1 behavior, Person 1 thoughts or feelings, Person 2 behavior, and/or Person 2 thoughts or feelings).
1. Offer a brief reflection that summarizes the elements of the cycle expressed thus far.
2. Ask an open-ended question to elicit more information about the other, unknown elements of the cycle.
